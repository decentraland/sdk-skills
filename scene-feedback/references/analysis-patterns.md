# Investigative patterns

Use these as possible lines of inquiry when interpreting collected responses, in any output
format. Select those relevant to the goal and available evidence. Report unsupported or
small-group observations as questions to investigate rather than established patterns.

- **Concentrated friction:** check whether a theme clusters by platform, scene version, or
  trigger. Show the relevant group sizes and account for different question wording or
  recruitment before attributing a difference to the platform or an update.
- **Same rating, different experiences:** distinguish reasons behind similar scores, such
  as unclear rules versus difficult controls. They may call for different investigations.
- **Rating/comment tension:** inspect high ratings containing serious complaints and low
  ratings containing praise. Preserve both signals instead of assigning one overall sentiment.
- **Repeat reports versus broad reach:** distinguish repeated reports by one respondent
  from independent people describing the same problem. Do not equate row count with reach.
- **Rare but severe incidents:** flag reports such as lost progress separately from frequent
  minor frustrations. State whether the incident is reported, reproduced, or corroborated.
- **Conflicting needs:** retain opposing preferences, such as too easy versus too hard.
  Explore meaningful cohorts where data supports them; a middle average may describe neither.

For a useful finding, connect **what was noticed → supporting answers and counts → what to
check next**. Keep contradictory evidence and plausible alternative explanations visible.
A suspected mechanism or proposed fix remains a hypothesis until checked.

## Find patterns without losing the answers

Read all relevant answers in manageable batches before making dataset-wide claims. For a
sampled review, state how the sample was selected and restrict claims to that sample.
Group semantically similar experiences even when their words differ. Distinguish a shared
symptom from an inferred cause, keep ambiguous answers visible, and retain disagreement.
A response may support several themes; disclose overlapping counts.

Keep a traceable mapping from each theme to response IDs and original comments. Count
unique respondents supporting a theme within the relevant cohort, and state the denominator.
Use “most” or “a majority” only when that count supports it; frequency alone does not measure
severity. Surface a rare serious issue separately when warranted. Treat proposed fixes as
hypotheses rather than facts established by complaints.

# Whole-game briefs — propose a game the owner can imagine

Use this route after hearing the seed, when the owner wants whole-game proposals or accepts an
offer to explore them. Offer a natural choice: develop the idea through questions, or sketch
several games they can choose, mix and change. The owner shapes the game in either route;
describe the choice as a way to work, not a trade of authorship for speed. Reuse supplied answers. A clear request already
chooses the route; a ready concept or wish to build needs no detour through a menu.

## Keep the attraction; vary the game

Identify what should survive from the owner's images, desired feeling and constraints. A named
predecessor may mean a loved action, a social situation, a mood or a whole loop; ask only if
that distinction is unclear and changes the proposals. Notice what is already specific rather
than treating a familiar genre as a defect.

Separate **creative direction** from **designer focus**. A close adaptation keeps the valued,
recognizable core and changes its delivery for this context. A spiritual successor keeps the
attractive core and develops the experience further; it is not an official sequel. Development
in depth adds expressive actions, interacting rules and mastery. Development in breadth adds
meaningful roles, relationships or situations, rather than just more content. A bold
reinterpretation may change mechanics, roles, loop or genre while keeping the desired experience.
These can overlap: an adaptation can broaden, and a successor need not add more roles.

Use three designer focuses as thinking tools, not personas the owner must manage:
- **Experience:** who the player becomes, what happens in play and what story they might tell.
  Let atmosphere, space, relationships and improvisation appear through actions and consequences.
- **Systems:** simple expressive actions and interacting rules; choices and mastery that stay
  interesting after repetition. Prefer connected mechanics to more content. Respect novice entry,
  handling and intentional solo play; complexity alone adds no value.
- **Audience:** who wants this experience, in what situation, why they might try it, and how play
  delivers the invitation. Use [acquisition](acquisition.md) for evidence and design implications.
  Apply this across proposals. When the owner starts from an audience or channel, let it lead
  the search. Otherwise it helps shape the games rather than dividing one unchanged game into
  friends, walk-ins and tournament versions. Different people alone do not establish different play.

For an agreed **broad menu**, use this six-brief starting set unless the owner's scope calls for
another count or distance. A requested three, six or three-plus-three takes precedence. This is
a way to generate contrast, not six required stages or six separate agents:

| Direction | Leading focus | Find a whole game around |
|---|---|---|
| One close adaptation | Experience + Systems | Recognizable pleasure and working mechanics in a fitting DCL situation |
| One successor in breadth | Experience | New meaningful roles and relationships |
| A second successor in breadth | Experience + Systems | A different expansion through space, session structure or links between situations/systems; distinguish its play from the first |
| One successor in depth | Systems | More expressive decisions and mastery from the familiar action set |
| One combination of breadth and depth | Experience + Systems | A new situation that lets mechanics develop greater depth; explain how the two support each other |
| One bold reinterpretation | Experience | The desired experience through a more freely changed activity, loop or genre |

For a new game without a predecessor, explore these distances from the owner's seed; do not
invent a required source game. For an explicitly close transfer, keep the agreed distance rather
than inserting unwanted reinventions. A rejection for one adaptation does not ban reinvention
after the owner changes the goal. Respect a current decline without repeating the offer.
Compare actual episodes: different names or settings may hide the same game. A distinction in
sound, attention or feeling counts when it changes play. Avoid sending every genre towards the
same co-op or hidden-information solution; preserve the owner's player mode and tone.

## Research at the chosen depth

Use [prior art](prior-art.md) when examples or an unknown could change the alternatives. A quick
scan can support this early choice; reuse relevant skill knowledge and supplied material, then
search useful gaps with sources and limits. Follow the agreed depth and research-delivery rules.
A menu does not itself authorize deep research into every proposal. Broader research can follow
the owner's choice or a request to explore further; it is optional, not a gate before design.
Without research, label proposals accordingly. An unusual combination or lack of known matches
does not prove originality, demand or fun; inspect fit and trade-offs rather than promising them.

## Deliver short briefs and an HTML view

The result at this point is a set of **short whole-game briefs**, not a full GDD for each game.
Write in the owner's language, with enough connected prose to understand the game without
reconstructing it from labels. Begin with three short bold inline cues, translated naturally:
**Idea → Episode → Loop**. The idea gives the fantasy
and player role. The episode unfolds a possible moment: what the player notices, chooses,
and what happens next. The loop makes the repeating sequence clear, including what leads
into the next round. Explain how the mechanics connect those choices and consequences;
keep useful detail after the loop. An episode illustrates proposed rules, not playtest evidence.

Give each brief a meaningful difference, a coherent first playable, player mode and the main
uncertainty or cost. For an adaptation, explain what survives and what changes. Preserve useful
connections to audience and references when they explain the game. Give each game one title
and compact flowing prose, with short bold inline cues where helpful. Paragraph breaks should
follow a change of thought, rather than turn every part into a separate headed section. The
remaining cues can vary with the game. Keep the larger vision distinct from the first cut.
Shortness comes from choosing useful detail, not removing the links that make the game
understandable.

Before presenting, read each brief as a newcomer: can they explain who they are, what they do,
what decision matters, what follows from it, and why they go again? Check that its rules work
together under the owner's player mode and tone. Revise an idea that depends on breaking a
stated condition. Keep unresolved rules and platform feasibility visible as unknowns rather
than inventing certainty to complete the story. This is a consistency check, not a playtest.

Save the short pending proposals in the growing `design/gdd.md`, with clear names and proposal
status. A separate brief document and complete template are unnecessary. Keep detailed research
in its existing home. In the HTML view, follow the briefs with a short comparison; give a reasoned recommendation
when invited, clearly separate from the owner's decision.

Also create a readable local **`design/concepts.html`** view and link it for the owner, unless
they decline HTML or the environment cannot write it. Use **progressive disclosure**:
- Group creative directions in tabs. For a three-plus-three request, use two: close adaptations
  and successors in breadth. Match the actual menu; a mixed six-brief set need not become 3+3,
  and a single group needs no extra tab.
- Within the selected group, show briefs sequentially on a shared reading surface: one game
  title followed by compact prose with inline bold cues. Use a responsive, generous desktop
  width and modest padding. A useful starting layout is a centered sheet up to about 1150 CSS px,
  with about 48 px inner padding and smaller gutters on narrow screens. Set the sheet width
  independently of font size; keep a single text column. Avoid separate padded cards
  or a heading and vertical gap for every part of a brief. Keep type and spacing comfortable
  at normal zoom, and reduce padding on small screens. Use the owner's supplied presentation
  as the visual reference. A compact comparison can follow the complete briefs.
- Provide a separate depth control: briefs first, then the current GDD/comparison and research
  or notes where those materials exist. Longer records can open in expandable sections. This
  lets the owner read deeper without making the initial choice longer. Use saved material;
  this presentation does not require full GDDs per variant or authorize additional research.

Carry the complete brief explanation into HTML, including the links between actions, choices
and consequences; do not replace it with a shorter card summary. Put supporting detail behind
the depth controls, while keeping the explanation of the game in the brief itself. Preserve
uncertainties and proposal status from the saved design.
Check that every brief is reachable, links and group/depth navigation work, and hidden content becomes available when
selected. This is a view of the saved design, not a second independent canon. Respect a different
layout requested by the owner.
**Deliver in both chat and HTML.** In chat, give each game a title and the **Idea → Episode → Loop**
portion of its complete brief. Preserve the connected explanation so the owner can understand
and compare the games directly in the conversation. Let the content set the length and paragraph
breaks; do not trim it to meet a sentence, word or paragraph count. Keep the full explanation, comparisons,
research and supporting detail in HTML and the saved design; the short chat version does not
replace them. Link the HTML after the chat briefs and invite a free-form choice or mix.
Respect a different delivery requested by the owner. If file creation or viewing is unavailable,
still give the chat briefs and name that limitation.

## Turn the owner's mix into one game

The owner may choose one proposal, take parts from several, add their own, or reject the set.
Use their whole reply rather than requiring a number. Preserve the desired parts and explain
material conflicts through the resulting episode. Propose connecting rules with their origin
visible; do not treat your repairs or an unchosen proposal as approved. Reuse what is already
known instead of reopening an interview for each mechanic.

Write the selected or combined direction into the active GDD, keeping proposals separate from
owner decisions. Preserve useful unchosen directions in `design/ideas.md` under the existing
[record homes](hypothesis-log/files.md#record-homes). After a choice or mix, make the HTML's
status consistent throughout: title, tabs, expanded sections and links. Keep the original
proposals available as a dated historical menu, with the current choice and a link to the
active GDD. Describe its old open questions as open **at the time of the menu**, not pending
now. A panel called "current GDD" must show the updated design or link to it; a preserved
snapshot needs a historical label. A banner alone does not fix stale claims inside a tab. Use
[player promise](player-promise.md) when useful to express the chosen game's appeal.

From that connected game, propose the smallest useful playable through
[first-playable guidance](interview-moves.md#keep-the-first-playable-in-view) and the existing
[handoff](hypothesis-log/handoff.md). Distinguish the larger direction from this first cut; mixing
is welcome, but does not commit every borrowed feature to the first build. Keep future possibilities
recoverable. Choosing a brief does not authorize a build or require deep research before one.

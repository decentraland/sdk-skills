# Acquisition — connect the game with its first players

Use when an audience, useful comparable or first playable is clear enough to connect people
with an experience. Reuse known answers and expose design opportunities or gaps. Deeper research
is optional, never a condition for building; a private gathering or one-shot game can be the goal.

## Enter from the game or the people

For an existing idea, start with its promise and what the owner wants to keep. Games found in
[prior art](prior-art.md) can lead to communities where people discuss and play that genre or
choose games for a similar occasion, beyond a single game's fan group. After a useful comparable
pass, offer an audience search if it would inform the next choice. When finding or comparing
communities or channels, read [audience discovery](audience-discovery.md) before the search,
including a direct request to investigate Reddit, Discord or TikTok. A mention of a game
is a lead, not proof that those people want this game or will move to Decentraland.

For a known community, start with its interests, meeting habits and desired experience; suggest
gameplay options and trade-offs before a mechanics search if useful. For a streamer, distinguish
the host's choices, the group's play and viewers' response. People and analogues can inform each
other without replacing the author's idea with a market niche. With neither game nor group,
[audience discovery](audience-discovery.md) can start from people.

Make the next step concrete: whose situation matters, why they might try this experience,
and what that changes in the first playable. Use known answers rather than an ICP questionnaire.
Offer research at a meaningful decision or changed evidence need; reuse the offer/decline rules
in [prior art](prior-art.md#offer-and-focus). A cosmetic change or resume does not reset a decline.
A direct request authorises the research, without another invitation.

## Research people as well as games

Read [research delivery](research-delivery.md) before the pass; it covers shared resources,
incremental records and reconciliation, including when this work is delegated.
Use the [search map and coverage review](prior-art.md#map-and-revisit-the-search) for a deep
pass, applied to people and play situations rather than only mechanics. Build an evidence-backed
set of audience and design options; a short recommendation should not erase the wider findings.

**Find groups through the reason they play.** Starting from analogues, follow where people
compare games, organise sessions, recommend alternatives, share mods or discuss frustrations.
Look beyond each game's fan group to shared genres, occasions and interests. Starting from a
known community, investigate what its members actually choose and do before selecting an analogue.
Group promising leads by situation and motivation; a group name or audience size alone does not
explain fit. Explore alternatives that could change the design, not more names for the same group.

**Inspect episodes, not only labels.** Find examples of how a session starts, who plays together,
what they do, what draws a response and why they stop or return. For a streamer, separate the
host's actions, the playing group's interaction and the viewers' visible response. Compare across
relevant episodes or titles when possible: does the apparent appeal recur, or depend on a specific
host, editing, novelty or group? Preserve disagreements and differences within a community rather
than combining them into a single persona. Choose the sample to answer the question, without a
fixed quota. Explain how missing or inaccessible material limits the answer. An accessible
neighbouring group or genre can provide a new lead, but does not substitute for the named audience;
state that change of scope and leave the original question open.

Follow relevant primary sources: community discussions and rules, developer accounts, and
observed player practices. Look for the context behind a preference: how people find company,
choose a session, explain a joke, or deal with an existing frustration. Keep the source, date,
locator and what it supports. Separate an observed practice, an owner's report and your inference;
a generated persona or a large community is not evidence of demand. Compare each candidate with
the game using only that candidate's stated preferences: what fits, conflicts or is unknown.
Keep that scope in summaries too; evidence about part of a group describes only that part.
For channel research, record the sample and time window. Distinguish full sessions from edited
highlights, a recurring game from a recurring appeal, and observed reactions from assumed reasons.
A community preference informs a design proposal; it does not itself set a requirement for
this game. Choosing an audience does not approve every preference reported about it.

**Check the recommendation against its sources.** Before handing back a research synthesis,
including a worker's report, trace each reason that favours or rules out an option to an
observation or an explicit design assumption. Check the premise as well as the conclusion:
a familiar format or genre label may suggest details that the source never established. Keep
those details conditional where they affect the advice, or recommend on a different supported
basis. A tentative recommendation does not make an invented premise factual.
Keep the measured thing, people, period and format attached to the observation. Counts of titles
are not counts of played games; an old archive leaves current activity open. Tool-generated page
summaries retain their extraction limits; a description summary is not an observed play session.

Distinguish **what ranked higher** from **why people chose or enjoyed it**. Views can support a
ranking within the inspected sample; compare age, format, sponsorship and the game's existing
popularity before using that ranking to explain audience preference. Where these effects cannot
be separated, keep the explanation tentative in the recommendation and comparison table too.
Ask yourself whether the design advice still makes sense under a plausible alternative explanation.
It may still be a promising, affordable first attempt; explain that design reason without claiming
the audience has validated it. A weak comparison can lower confidence without eliminating an
otherwise useful option. This need not trigger more research or delay the owner's choice.

If a chosen analogue's key mechanic rests on a secondary description, check its primary source
when accessible; otherwise keep that part provisional. Narrow unsupported claims where they
influence the choice, rather than relying on a general disclaimer elsewhere. This is your
synthesis work, not another questionnaire or a required research round.

**Carry observations into design advice.** Show the useful connection: what people did or said,
the appeal it suggests, a playable moment we could offer, and what the adaptation keeps or changes.
Consider another explanation where it would change the advice. Then recommend an option with
its reason and limits, without waiting for another request for an opinion. Enjoyment of
coordinating friends suggests a different design from watching a host struggle, even around
the same game. Keep alternatives and later opportunities with their evidence and trade-offs in
the linked research; the owner chooses what the current game serves. Check whether one accessible
channel has crowded out other relevant situations or contrary evidence before proposing more work.

Reddit is one possible source, not the default channel for every game.
Local language, humour or shared culture may help an experience fit; check the actual context
rather than assuming everyone in a group is alike. Verify unexplained shorthand or leave it open.

Use [platform fit](platform-fit.md) for load-bearing access or feature claims. A player already
in DCL and someone new to the platform may face different paths. Check the relevant link,
client/device, access and required company when these affect the invitation; a possible
workaround stays a candidate until checked. Reading about a channel does not authorise
contacting its members, publishing, purchasing ads or collecting their data.

## Turn attraction into playable choices

Select the question that matters now, explain its consequence, and offer concrete alternatives:

- **Promise and experience:** what can the intended player recognise or want, and which ordinary
  playable moment delivers it? A compelling clip can attract spectators without attracting players.
- **Company and arrival:** what happens when the first person arrives alone, or friends arrive at
  different times? A hosted session, different roles or a solo activity may fit; bots or solo play
  are not mandatory fixes for a group game.
- **Sharing and participation:** what would someone naturally show or invite a friend to do?
  A readable outcome, a useful role or a shared story can matter more than a referral reward.
  Keep the reason to share, the friend's entry path and the promised experience distinct.
- **Watching and playing:** consider readability, downtime, spoilers and spectator participation
  if streamers matter to this audience. Public sharing need not suit every game. Production of
  clips belongs to promotion; a design decision can make real moments easier to capture later.

For the selected direction, sketch a plausible path **from a specific place and invitation to
the first promised action**. Name the community or channel where it could start, the suitable
format and occasion, what would make these people try, and the steps they take to reach
the promised moment in the game. Save the proposed joining steps in `design/acquisition.md`,
naming **who acts, where, and what the other person gets next**. For an arranged session, this
includes where a player responds and where the organiser gives them the time and join link,
then who meets them and how a party starts. For immediate play, follow the link through entry
to the first action. A reader should be able to follow the route without inventing an unmentioned
reply channel or delivery step. Propose a simple arrangement that fits the community's rules;
keep it a proposal until chosen. Unknown dates, URLs and untested access can stay open while
the way to join is concrete. This is a short route, not a signup system to build or an owner
questionnaire.
A subreddit discussion, a short TikTok video and an invitation
to a Discord game night are different routes, not interchangeable distribution slots. Choose
from the actual findings; explain why the place and format fit this audience and play situation.
Keep the community link, relevant posting rules and access or host approval beside the route
when known; mark missing evidence as an open check. A discovered community is a lead, not an
available placement. These are usable route proposals, not finished posts or a mandatory channel
list. Point out the transition most likely to lose the promise and suggest a proportionate
design response.
For example, a clip of friends failing together may promise company that an empty lobby cannot
provide; an arranged session is one possible response, not a requirement for every game.
Keep the route aligned with the owner's goal. To reach new players, identify a relevant external
community and a reason for its members to try this game. Players bringing their own friends can
be a sharing loop; explain how the first players discover the game. A private gathering remains
valid when that is itself the owner's goal.
**Keep the baseline circle separate from external acquisition.** Friends, family and existing
Decentraland communities, Discords, events and discovery channels can fill early sessions and
start sharing. Mention them once when useful and record them separately in `design/acquisition.md`;
availability and permission need checking, and their presence does not establish demand.
They neither count as external candidates or requested-channel coverage nor replace finding
new players. Classify by people reached: a DCL Discord or subreddit is internal. Follow an
explicit goal to reach existing DCL players and label it internal acquisition; otherwise keep
research and its main recommendation external, even if internal playtests happen first.
Choosing only baseline playtests is valid but leaves external acquisition open.
An unconfirmed host or community remains a possible route. Name the dependency and a proportionate
next check. If offering an alternative, keep the same acquisition goal and use relevant leads;
unknown access stays an open check, not a reason to substitute a private playtest.
Keep device, link and access details that need checking explicit. A few lines can be enough;
this neither requires a campaign plan before building nor authorises outreach. Ask about a choice
only when it changes the owner's design.

When choosing a playable option, check which audience benefit depends on the parts changed in
adaptation. Friends laughing over voice chat in a horror game is a lead for shared suspense;
a solo adaptation needs its own reason to appeal. Offer alternatives that respect the owner's
scope. A channel's interest also leaves its willingness to feature the game and viewers' willingness
to play it unknown. Keep the evidence and these limits beside the proposal in acquisition.
Familiar solutions can be chosen for implementation; use a hypothesis only for a consequential uncertainty or an
owner-chosen experiment. Neither another game's success nor a paper walkthrough proves uptake.

## Let reaching players inform the game

When the audience and a plausible channel are specific enough, offer a small check of what
would make the invitation work. Also use this check when the owner brings an ad draft or
feedback from someone preparing promotion. The useful direction is **target players → what
reaching them requires → a game incentive or mechanic**. It can inform an unfinished design;
it need not wait for a build or a campaign. Respect a decline until a material new question arises.

For a trend or short-video lead, identify the activity people recognise and the action,
choice and consequence that could make it playable. A popular subject alone is not a mechanic.
[Supersonic's trend-to-game process](https://supersonic.com/?p=80573) offers examples from
hyper-casual games; its historical view and acquisition thresholds are not targets for this game.

When testing a showable moment, distinguish interest in watching from wanting to play. Show
it to people who could become players, not only fellow developers; then compare what they
expected with what happens when they try it. [Tiny Glade's developers](https://80.lv/articles/exclusive-tiny-glade-developers-discuss-bevy-proceduralism-publishers-cozy-games)
used public clips and playtests for these different questions. For sharing, consider a result
players make themselves as well as a funny reaction: [Opus Magnum](https://www.zachtronics.com/opus-magnum/)
lets players export their solutions as GIFs. Propose the equivalent reason to share for this
game; verify capture or export support before promising it. These are optional design approaches,
not evidence that a channel will bring players or that the game needs a sharing feature.

Start with the actual place, format and play situation: what could these people see or understand,
why would they want to take part, and what must the game deliver? A short proposed invitation or
description of a showable moment can expose a gap before production. Label imagined material as
a concept. Use the production handoff below for finished promotional assets.

Work out what the difficulty means before proposing a fix. Is an existing benefit hard to
explain or show? Does the channel reach people with a different interest or joining situation?
Or does the game lack the action, consequence or reason to participate that the invitation needs?
An unclear draft is a clue, not proof that the mechanic is wrong. Compare the feedback with the
current design and its evidence; keep requirements reported by a marketer distinct from observed
player behaviour or verified channel rules.

Offer proportionate alternatives with their trade-offs: change the message or route, reconsider
the audience, or change the playable experience. An incentive may be curiosity, a meaningful
role, an expressive choice, a challenge or a shared joke; a reward system is only one possibility.
Show how a proposed mechanic serves this audience and what it costs the owner's intended game.
Preserving the mechanic and improving its presentation can be the right outcome. Follow the
owner's choice rather than treating promotional feedback as authority over their design.

After a choice, use the recording guidance below to preserve the requirement, its source and
limits, the considered responses and the chosen change. Update affected GDD passages as well as
acquisition. Revisit the invitation and the first promised action: does the revised game supply
the reason to try, and can this format communicate it honestly? Name what still needs a player
or channel check; before any build, an optional
audience check can ask the chosen people. Use `dcl-prototype` if installed, or prepare a
[handoff](hypothesis-log/handoff.md) naming who to ask, what to show and what the response can establish.
A coherent draft establishes neither demand nor a successful campaign.
This is a useful design iteration, not a mandatory approval gate or a reason to repeat the
exercise on every turn. Stop or return to other design work when the owner chooses.

## Keep a separate working document

When there is acquisition work to save, maintain `design/acquisition.md`. It holds the current
audiences and situations, evidence and assumptions, promise and candidate routes to players,
design implications, observations and the next useful check. These are topics to grow as needed,
not mandatory headings or an empty template. **Save enough for another agent to use the work
without this conversation:**

- Keep both the broad audience and the concrete play situations the design serves: who comes,
  with whom, on what occasion, and what they want from that session. Link the relevant communities
  and explain their fit; preserve differences between groups rather than flattening them into a label.
- Save the routes described above, their reasons and their status: proposed, chosen, checked or
  still dependent on access. Preserve useful alternative communities, formats and later opportunities
  in linked research even when only one route is selected for the current game.
- Keep substantial research in `design/research/`, indexed through `design/references.md` and
  linked from acquisition. Preserve source URLs, dates and available locators such as video
  timestamps or discussion sections, what they support, and extraction limits. Retain the findings
  and trade-offs, not just a bibliography or the final recommendation. Reuse existing research files.
- Record the design implications and what a later promotion pass could work with: the promise,
  proposed invitation, relevant game moments, known assets and access or spoiler constraints.
  Distinguish intended, built, observed and allowed-to-show material; leave unknowns visible.

Save this as the work develops, including when the session ends before a build or promotion
request. Organise what is already known; missing details do not create an interview checklist
or require another research pass. Preserve replaced assumptions and reasons in the existing
decision/research history; keep the current view readable.

Keep `design/gdd.md` compact for concept review, growing from a brief towards a ten-pager rather
than a marketing archive; ten-pager is a shape, not a fixed page gate. Summarise only the selected
implications for the concept and first playable, with a link to acquisition. Keep a material
unresolved design question visible there; detailed candidates and findings stay in acquisition.
An early audience-first session can start both documents small. Review-only still leaves files
unchanged. On selection, resume or pivot, rebuild the current summary and next check from the
selected original findings and the owner's choices, rather than reusing an earlier combined
comparison. Revise the affected passages and choice labels in both records. Move earlier
comparisons and unchosen candidates with their conditions to research/history; they remain
available without setting requirements or next steps for the chosen game.

Before handing back a substantial update or pausing, read back the affected current passages
against the source observations and latest owner choices. Replace answered "waiting for research"
notes with the result and the uncertainty that actually remains. Check that proposed omissions
have not become agreed scope, and that a known subgroup has not become the whole audience.
Correct the current records in place while preserving the old reasoning in history. This is a
consistency pass on your own work, not another approval round or a requirement to finish the GDD.

## Choose evidence for the owner's goal

When the next step may involve asking players, use [player feedback](player-feedback.md)
before drafting player-facing wording. Keep a learning aim or proposed observation method in
the plan; that reference controls the move from planning evidence to preparing questions.

Name the action being learned about: noticing an invitation, choosing to try, entering the
scene, reaching the promised action, returning or bringing a friend. Choose an observable event
that matches the question; arriving together is not the same as starting shared gameplay.
Label a proxy and what it leaves unknown. Record counts, denominator,
time window, invitation context and measurement gaps when results exist. Keep unmatched counts
separate; do not invent links, missing visitors or excluded populations to explain them.
New to a scene does not mean new to DCL, and a click does not establish play. A tagged link is
an attribution proposal until the relevant event capture and linkage are verified. Do not promise
that it identifies scene visitors; a manual observation or self-report may answer a smaller question.

Draw a useful, bounded conclusion against the owner's goal from the available observations.
A qualitative goal can have qualitative evidence; numerical targets are optional choices, not
a prerequisite for learning from a session. Preserve a registered experiment's agreed criterion.
A seasonal story and a monthly group session need not optimise the same return window. Check
current analytics definitions before using their labels; benchmarks are not general design gates.
Distinguish observed actions from player reports; a proposed self-report remains a proposal.
Keep detailed observations in acquisition or the linked trial; update the GDD only with their
supported design implications, preserving any registered experiment's criterion and limits.

## Leave reusable input for promotion

The saved design files are the handoff; no separate promotion brief or invocation is needed
to preserve the work. `design/acquisition.md` is the entry point for audience and routes,
`design/gdd.md` for the current game, and `design/references.md` for the linked research library.
Keep those links usable so a later promotion agent can recover the choices, evidence and open
checks without repeating the interview or research. The recording guidance above owns this content.

Bring findings from a later promotion pass back through [the design check above](#let-reaching-players-inform-the-game)
when they reveal a gap in appeal or delivery; preparation and production can inform each other.
When the owner asks to produce promotional material, use the available promotion skill and
its current instructions for captions, capture, renders, videos and kit pages. Point it to these
files and clarify only missing information needed for the requested output. It may help before
release; permission to show a labelled concept can precede a build, while a planned feature is
not footage of existing gameplay. If the skill is unavailable, retain these inputs without
inventing its commands or capabilities. Saving design work alone starts no capture, installation,
scene changes, outreach or publication, and adds no Creator Success proposal requirement.
